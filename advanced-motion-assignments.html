The Animated Infographic

One concept, explained in motion. Thirty to sixty seconds to make a viewer understand something they didn't before.

20 points · Due Sunday, October 25, 2026 · 11:59 PM · Submit via Google Drive

The brief

Pick a concept. Make it understandable. You have 30 to 60 seconds, one clear idea, and every technique from Week 1 — if your movement doesn't mean something, it's decoration.

The assignment

Create a 30–60 second animated infographic about a topic of your choice. The piece must explain a concept — how something works, why something happens, how things compare, how a system fits together. Not a logo sting, not a vibes loop, not a chart that wiggles: a short visual explanation that teaches a first-time viewer something true.

Choose something with a real idea inside it. The best topics have a relationship worth revealing — growth, comparison, sequence, cause and effect, flow, hierarchy. If you can't say what your piece argues in one sentence, the topic isn't ready yet.

Work the process (from the Week 1 lesson)

  1. Find the idea. Not "here are statistics about X" — an argument: "X is accelerating faster than Y." One sentence.
  2. Identify the relationships. Comparison? Growth? Sequence? Cause and effect? That's what you'll actually be animating.
  3. Choose a visual model and define your visual language. Chart, map, timeline, system, metaphor, simulation — and decide what color, size, shape, and motion each mean in your piece. Write it down before you keyframe anything.
  4. Design the reveal. What does the viewer know at 0 seconds? At 10? At 30? At the end? Progressive disclosure is the whole game at this length.
  5. Animate relationships, not objects. Animate causality, comparison, growth, connections, transformations. For every move, you should be able to answer: "what does this movement mean?"

Use an array of techniques

Your piece should draw on several of the tools from class — the ones that fit your idea. A single looping bar chart won't cut it.

  • Visual grammar: size, color, position, and shape carrying meaning — consistently.
  • Metaphor & analogy: make the unfamiliar familiar (stadiums filling, chairs disappearing, the ladder of abstraction).
  • Progressive disclosure: reveal in the order the viewer can absorb it.
  • Camera with a purpose: moves that navigate, compare, or reveal — not fly-throughs for their own sake.
  • Data-driven motion: counters, growing forms, trim paths, procedural systems.
  • Transformations & morphs: one thing becoming another to show a relationship.
  • Kinetic typography: type that paces and emphasizes the argument.
  • Sound, if you use it: music/SFX that supports the reveal — optional, never a crutch.

Need a reference point? Revisit the Watch & Study page — steal a move from Vox, a camera idea from Johnny Harris, a metaphor from PolyMatter. Steal the technique, not the topic.

Movie Titles: The Title Card & the Credits Sequence

Before the story starts, the titles have already told you what kind of movie this is. Now design that moment — twice.

30 points · Due Sunday, October 25, 2026 · 11:59 PM · Submit via Google Drive

The brief

Two projects, one skill: making type carry a world. A title card that lands the film's name like a punch — and a credits sequence that walks the audience into the movie before it starts.

Project 1 — Animated Title Card (under 10 seconds)

The title card is the film's name as an event — the card that appears at the head of the movie and announces what you're watching. Think of the Harry Potter title materializing out of darkness and storm-light, or the Iron Man title built like the suit itself: industrial, mechanical, heavy. In both cases the name isn't just read — it's performed.

Your job: pick a film (real or invented) and design its title card. In under 10 seconds, the card has to establish the film's identity, tone, and world — before a single scene plays. Everything is a decision:

  • Typography: the letterforms themselves are the image. Choose (or build) type that belongs to this film and no other.
  • Material & texture: is the title carved, cast in metal, written in light, scratched into film? Material is meaning.
  • Light & camera: how the title is revealed — a move, a flicker, an assembly, a strike.
  • One decisive reveal: a title card gets one big idea. Build everything around it.
  • Sound (optional): a single hit, swell, or sting can double the impact — keep it subordinate to the visual idea.

Short is not the same as simple. Ten seconds of screen time, zero wasted frames.

Project 2 — Opening or Closing Credits Sequence (30–60 seconds)

The credits sequence is the journey into (or out of) the film. While it introduces the cast, director, and crew, its real job is to prepare you for what you're about to experience — tone, world, and rules. Think of Se7en: scratchy, obsessive, disturbed — you know exactly what kind of movie you're in before it starts. The original X-Men: a camera journey through a world of science and mutation. Spider-Man: swinging through a comic-book city with the credits woven into the environment itself.

Your job: design a full credits sequence for a film (real or invented — it can be the same film as your title card, or a different one). Requirements:

  • Real credits content: include the principal cast, director, and key crew roles (writer, producer, cinematographer, editor, composer…). Use the film's actual credits or invent plausible ones — no lorem-ipsum names.
  • A journey, not a slideshow: the sequence should take the viewer through something — a world, a process, a mood — with a beginning, a build, and an arrival.
  • Type integrated with imagery: credits that live in the sequence's world and visual system, not captions pasted over footage.
  • Tone match: a first-time viewer should be able to guess the film's genre and feel from your sequence alone.
  • Pacing: cuts, reveals, and credit appearances timed with intent — to music, if you use it.

3D Camera Tracking in Blender

Track the shot. Solve the camera. Refine until it locks. Then put something in the world and prove it stays there.

10 points · Due Sunday, October 25, 2026 · 11:59 PM · Submit via Google Drive

The brief

This one is about process, not pretty. After Effects' 3D Camera Tracker hides the work behind a button — Blender makes you do it yourself. Track the shot, solve the camera, refine until the object locks. That's the whole assignment.

Your footage & tutorial

The shot is provided as an image sequence (150 frames) — download it here:

https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1VcZBVrcPo4vCC8jnjjU373sl9UyBXCEl?usp=drive_link

It's the same footage used in this walkthrough — "A Beginner's Guide to Camera Tracking in Blender 4.0" — follow along with it if you get stuck: youtube.com/watch?v=ui0JUHE12k8

The workflow

  1. Set up. In Blender's Movie Clip Editor, load the image sequence and set your scene length to the full shot: 150 frames. Make sure the clip is set as the camera background so you can see your scene over the footage.
  2. Apply track points. Place markers on distinct, high-contrast features that stay visible — corners, edges, texture details. Spread them across the frame and across depth: foreground and background points give the solver the parallax it needs. You need at least 8 solid tracks; more good tracks beat fewer perfect ones. Track them through the entire shot.
  3. Solve the camera. Run Solve Camera Motion and read your solve error. That number is your report card — a high error means the solve isn't trustworthy yet.
  4. Refine — this is the real work. Find the markers with the worst error. Clean them up, re-track them, or delete the bad ones entirely (a few lying markers will poison the whole solve). Re-solve. Repeat until the error is low and the camera move feels like the footage.
  5. Orient the scene. Use your solved markers to set the floor (ground plane), a wall plane, the origin, and a sensible scene scale. This is what makes your object sit in the world instead of floating near it.
  6. Place your object. Add a 3D object — any object you want, or several. It does not have to be the default cube. Align it to the ground/wall planes so it belongs in the space.
The test your work has to pass

Play or scrub the full 150 frames and watch your object, not the footage. It must stay locked to the camera move: no slippage, no sliding, no swimming — sitting on the ground plane at frame 1, frame 75, and frame 150. If it drifts, the assignment isn't done; go back to refining. A locked mediocre object beats a beautiful object that slides.
