Part 1

The anatomy of a brief that works

The 8 parts go on the board as a column; they stay up all day.

Anatomy

Eight parts, eight failure modes

Role · Context · Constraints · References
Avoid · Output format · Criteria · The decision line

Remove any one → a specific, predictable kind of bad output.

Teaching move: for each part ask "what happens without it?" BEFORE revealing. They can derive most from Week 1 memory.

Anatomy 1/8

Role

"Act as a practical creative director for motion design."

Without it: generic assistant voice, generic standards.

Role sets the bar the model aims at. "Practical" is doing work in that sentence — it suppresses fluff.

Anatomy 2/8

Context

Project, audience, where the work lives.

Without it: the model invents a default context. The default is wrong.

"Narrow audience beats broad: 'Dublin gig-goers who already like synth music' outperforms 'everyone' every time."

Anatomy 3/8

Constraints

Format, palette, duration, budget, the non-negotiables.

Without them: you get the average of the internet.

Full constraints section coming — this is the headline.

Anatomy 4/8

References

The qualities you want — described precisely, or shown.

Without them: "style" collapses into cliché.

The consent line gets its own slide shortly.

Anatomy 5/8

The avoid list

What you explicitly don't want.

Without it: the clichés walk right in wearing lanyards.

Cheapest upgrade in prompting. "No neon, no rain, no hooded figures" does visible work.

Anatomy 6/8

Output format

"Five directions, each: one-line concept, layout in words, one risk."

Without it: one long, confident essay.

Output shape keeps the model from deciding for you — "directions + risks" forces options open.

Anatomy 7/8

Criteria

How drafts will be judged — written into the brief.

Without them: you can't critique what has no standard. Neither can the model.

These become the rubric in the lab. Plant the seed now.

Anatomy 8/8

The decision line

"Don't pick a winner — that's my job."

Without it: the model decides. You rubber-stamp.

"Eight words that keep you the director. Make them a habit."

Reality check

Do you need all eight every time?

No. Quick tasks: role + ask.

But when output disappoints, the diagnosis is always:
"Which organ is missing?"

That's the lab.

Part 1, continued

References: show, don't just tell

~0:38.

References

Three levels

  1. Telling: "1970s print feeling" — okay
  2. Telling precisely: "flat colour, heavy grain, slight misregistration, like a 1970s riso gig poster" — much better
  3. Showing: paste 1–3 examples of what good looks like — fastest taste transfer
Level 2 vocabulary: materials, light, colour behaviour, texture, type feeling. Level 3 = "few-shot."
Course rule

References

The consent line

We do not prompt with living artists' names.

✓ Qualities: "Bauhaus poster geometry"
✗ Signature theft: "in the style of [working illustrator]"

Deliver carefully: consent fight centre + professionally radioactive + community trust. "This appears in every integrity check this term."

The craft argument

Why qualities beat names (besides ethics)

Naming qualities forces you to understand
why you like the reference.

That understanding works on every brief, every tool, every collaborator, forever.

The craft argument lands with students the ethics argument doesn't reach. Use both.

Part 1, finale

The critique loop

Your hostile reviewer on demand

~0:44. "Highest-value prompting skill. Needs zero generation."

Critique loops

Without criteria: compliments

The model is trained to be liked.
The word is sycophancy.

"This is a strong concept with great potential!" — about everything.

Have a real saccharine example ready from prep. The room laughs, then gets it.

Critique loops

With your rubric: a second pair of eyes

Work + your criteria + "be specific" + you decide

= competent criticism at 2am before the deadline

"The model doesn't replace crit. It makes you arrive at crit with the obvious problems already fixed."

Critique loops

The loop, four steps

  1. Give it the work (text or screenshot)
  2. Give it your criteria (from your brief)
  3. Demand: the weakness, why it matters for this audience, one concrete fix
  4. You decide what to accept — filtering critique is taste training
Step 4 is the integrity step. Accepting everything = outsourcing taste.
Try this now

90 seconds

The anti-sycophancy clause

Do not compliment me. Do not soften. If the work is generic, say where and why. Give me the review a tired, skeptical creative director would give at 6pm on a Friday.
Students paste it onto anything they have. The tone change is immediate and memorable.

Habits

Iteration etiquette

  • React with specifics, not "make it better"
  • One variable at a time when close; start over when not
  • Fresh chats for fresh directions
  • Three failed revisions = the brief has a hole
  • Briefs live in docs, never only in chats
Five habits, one slide. The handout repeats them.

The closing move

From brief to rubric

Build me a 6-line critique rubric. Each line: one pass/fail question tied to a SPECIFIC constraint in this brief. No generic design-school questions.

Then: reject any line that could apply to any project.

Reject at least one line live and make it regenerate. The rejection is the teachable moment.

Lab A

The discipline

Predict first

  1. Predict what's wrong before running (anatomy table)
  2. Run it. Were you right?
  3. Fix the two weakest organs only
  4. Re-run. Save before/after for share-out
Enforce predict-first hard. Teams fixing everything at once: "Two organs. Which two?"
Lab B · 25 min

Solo

Your brief, your ✏️ marks

Full skeleton, real current project. Run. One surgery pass.

Mark ✏️ every line only you could have written — audience knowledge, taste, constraints.

The ✏️ marks are authorship evidence. No project? Imaginary band/show/app — but push for real.
Lab C · 20 min

Solo

Your rubric must bite

Six lines from YOUR brief's constraints.

Test on Part B's output: at least two lines must fail.
All passing? Sharpen the rubric, not your feelings.

"A rubric that everything passes is a greeting card."
