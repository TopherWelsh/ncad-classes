Why this matters in real work

The skill gap this term isn't "who knows the magic words" — it's who can give creative direction. Briefing is an art director's craft, and everything here also makes you better at briefing printers, photographers, and collaborators. The AI is just the cheapest, most patient practice partner you'll ever get.

Kill this myth now

There are no magic words. "Prompt engineering" for creative work is ~90% classic creative-brief discipline — something agencies have done for a century. The other 10% is machine quirks, covered below.

02Key Concepts

1 · Prompting is briefing, not spellcasting

PartWhat it doesWithout it

ConstraintsFormat, palette, duration, non-negotiablesThe average of the internet
ReferencesQualities you want — shown or described"Style" becomes cliché
Avoid listWhat you explicitly don't wantThe clichés walk right in
Output format"5 directions, each with a risk"One long confident essay
CriteriaHow drafts will be judgedNothing to critique against

2 · Constraints are creative direction

Counterintuitive but reliable: more constraints → more interesting output. "Poster, any style" returns the global average. Tight constraints force the model into corners — and corners are where interesting work lives.

Watch out

Models confidently ignore constraints they can't satisfy (exact word counts, arithmetic, precise layouts). Constrain direction; verify compliance yourself. Tip: "list my constraints back before answering."

3 · References: show, don't just tell

  1. Telling: "1970s print feeling" — okay.
  2. Telling precisely: "flat colour, heavy grain, slight misregistration, like a 1970s risograph gig poster" — much better. Name qualities: materials, light, colour behaviour, texture, type feeling.
  3. Showing (few-shot): paste 1–3 examples of what good looks like. The fastest taste transfer.
The consent line — course rule

We don't prompt with living artists' names. Describe qualities; use historical movements as qualities. Beyond the ethics: naming qualities forces you to understand why you like a reference — and that understanding is the transferable skill.

4 · The critique loop: your hostile reviewer on demand

The highest-value prompting skill — it works even if you never generate anything. Work + your criteria + a demand for specifics + your decision about what to accept. A model with no criteria gives compliments (the word is sycophancy). A model holding your rubric gives you a second pair of eyes at 2am.

5 · Iteration etiquette

  • React with specifics — "No. 2 is closest but the tone is corporate; push it stranger."
  • One variable at a time when close; start over when not.
  • Fresh chats for genuinely different directions (context anchoring is real).
  • Three failed revisions in a row = the brief has a hole.
  • Briefs live in docs, never only inside chats.

05Step-by-Step Workflows

Prompt surgery (the diagnostic loop)

  1. Run once; read closely. What kind of bad? Generic = missing constraints/references. Wrong direction = missing context. Essay wall = missing output format. Bossy = missing decision line.
  2. Name the missing organ using the anatomy table.
  3. Transplant it in. Add the one missing part. Re-run.
  4. Compare side by side. Note what changed — your evidence trail.
  5. One more pass. Two surgical passes beat ten random tweaks.

Build your track rubric (10 minutes)

  1. Take your brief's constraints and goal.
  2. Convert each into a pass/fail question. ("Must work as a phone story" → "Does type survive at story-size?")
  3. Add the course's five standing critique questions (rubric template).
  4. Test on any AI output. If a mediocre draft passes everything, sharpen until it bites.

06Prompt Recipes

The full brief skeletonROLE: Act as a practical [creative director / design director / story editor] for my discipline: [track]. PROJECT: [what's being made, for what occasion] AUDIENCE: [who actually encounters it — be narrow] GOAL: [what they should think/feel/do — one sentence] CONSTRAINTS: [format, dimensions, colours, duration, budget, deadline, non-negotiables] REFERENCES: [2–3 named QUALITIES you want, not living artists] AVOID: [clichés, competitor looks, anything off-limits] OUTPUT: [exact shape: e.g., 5 directions, each with one-line concept, layout in words, one risk] Don't pick a winner — that's my job. Ask up to 3 clarifying questions first if anything is unclear.
The rubric builderHere is my creative brief: [paste]. Build me a 6-line critique rubric. Each line: one pass/fail question I should ask of any draft, tied to a SPECIFIC constraint or goal in this brief. No generic design-school questions — if a line could apply to any project, replace it.
The track adapterHere is my base brief: [paste]. Rewrite it for a [motion designer / illustrator / UX designer / 3D artist] producing [specific deliverable]. Change only what must change: deliverable specs, constraints, references, critique criteria. Highlight what you changed and why.
The prompt diagnosticianHere is a prompt I wrote and the output it produced: [paste both]. Diagnose the prompt like an art director reviewing a bad brief: which of these is missing or weak — role, context, constraints, references, output format, criteria, decision ownership? Rewrite the prompt fixing only the weakest two parts.
The anti-sycophancy clauseDo not compliment me. Do not soften. If the work is generic, say where and why. I am asking for the review a tired, skeptical creative director would give at 6pm on a Friday.

07Lab: Surgery → Brief → Rubric (70 min)

Cold start friendly — Week 1 attendance not required.

Part A — Prompt surgery, in teams (25 min)

Teams of 2–3. Three genuinely bad prompts. For each: predict what's wrong before running → run → fix the two weakest organs only → re-run → save before/after.

The patients
  • "Design me a logo for my brand. Make it modern and clean."
  • "Write something for my portfolio about my project."
  • "Give me ideas for my animation."

Part B — Your AI-ready brief (25 min, solo)

Full skeleton for a real current project. Run it; one surgery pass. Then mark ✏️ every line only you could have written — your audience knowledge, your taste, your constraints. That's the authorship evidence.

Part C — Your track rubric (20 min)

Six lines from your brief. Test on Part B's output: at least two lines must fail. If everything passes, sharpen the rubric, not your feelings.

Zero-generation track

Part A as paper diagnosis (predict, don't run). Parts B and C are writing tasks — the actual deliverable — with the model as optional critic only. Equal credit.

08Integrity Check

This week's four
  1. Whose taste is in the brief? If the model wrote your references and you kept them unread — whose direction is this?
  2. Are your references qualities, or a living artist's name wearing a trench coat?
  3. If this brief produces portfolio work, your disclosure note starts now: "Brief and direction mine; AI used for [x]."
  4. Briefing with a classmate's project or client-confidential material? Permission first.
