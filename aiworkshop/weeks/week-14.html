01This Week at a Glance

The week in one line

Ship one true thing that gives you leverage without taking your voice. Thirteen weeks of techniques are worth nothing until one lives in your practice. The trap to avoid: automating your art instead of your friction.

02The Capstone Quality Bar

Your capstone passes if it answers "yes" to all four. Print these; check them at the end.

  • Real friction. It solves an actual recurring problem in your practice — not a hypothetical, not someone else's.
  • Documented human decisions. Selection, rejection, refinement, and judgment are visible in the process explanation.
  • Honest disclosure. It states clearly what AI did and what you did.
  • Demo-able to an employer. You could show it in an interview and explain every choice without hand-waving.
The rule

A capstone that's small, finished, and passes all four beats an ambitious half-built thing every time.

04The Build Sprint (today's structure)

  1. Scope ruthlessly: name the friction, pick the shape, cut scope to ~6 hours of work total.
  2. Riskiest assumption first: what sinks the whole capstone if it fails? Test that first, not the easy parts.
  3. Build the spine: get the core working end-to-end, ugly. A rough complete thing beats a polished fragment.
  4. Clinic: a classmate tests whether it actually saves time or improves output.
  5. Refine + document: fix what the clinic found; write the process explanation as you go.
  6. Prep the 90-second show: what it solves, the decision you're proudest of, AI vs. you.

05The Scoping Conversation

Run this with AI, then run the same scope past a human — humans catch the "still over-scoped" the model is too agreeable to push on.

The capstone advisorAct as a capstone advisor. My project idea: [your idea]. Interview me briefly, then output a one-week build plan: scope cut to what's achievable in ~6 hours, the AI-role/human-role split, the riskiest assumption to test first, and what "done" looks like. Be ruthless about scope — I'd rather finish something small than half-build something big.

06The 1-Page Process Explanation (the assessed deliverable)

This document is what makes the capstone yours. One page, these sections:

  • The friction — the real recurring problem, with honest frequency.
  • What I built — the workflow/tool/system, in two sentences.
  • AI's role — specifically what AI did (drafted, generated, structured, automated).
  • My role — the decisions, selections, rejections, and craft that are yours. The ✏️ moments.
  • Proof it works — your acceptance check or time-saved estimate; ideally a clinic-partner's verdict.
  • Disclosure — the honest line you'd attach if this went in a portfolio.
  • What's next — the one thing you'd improve with more time.

Template starting points: workflow spec, tiny-tool spec, process documentation.

07Lab: Build Sprint + Workflow Clinic (the whole session)

  1. Scope (Hour 1): scoping conversation (AI + human); lock your shape and 6-hour scope; name your riskiest assumption.
  2. Sprint 1 (Hour 2): build the spine — riskiest assumption first, then core end-to-end. Ugly is fine.
  3. Workflow Clinic (~20 min): swap with a classmate; they actually try your thing. Does it save time / improve output? Would they use it? Where did it break?
  4. Sprint 2 (Hour 3): fix what the clinic found; write the process explanation as you go.
  5. Show-and-tell + peer review (Hour 4): 90-second rough shows; critique on ethics, usefulness, craft — not polish.
Zero-AI / planning capstone (fully equal)

A fully-specified workflow or tool design — the complete spec + process explanation + a paper walkthrough — is a legitimate capstone. Several of the strongest capstones are plans for things students will build later.

Came in cold today?

Pick the smallest shape (a prompt kit or tiny-tool spec), use a standing friction, and aim for "finished and honest" over "ambitious." A complete small capstone is a real capstone.

08Integrity Check

This week's four
  1. Does your capstone automate your friction or your art? If it's making your finished work, re-scope.
  2. Are your human decisions documented, not just claimed? Point to the ✏️ moments.
  3. Is your disclosure honest and specific enough to stand in an interview?
  4. Did a real human (clinic partner) confirm it does what you say? Untested "it saves time" is a hypothesis.
