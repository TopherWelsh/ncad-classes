02Key Concepts

1 · An LLM is a prediction machine, not a knowledge machine

The model inside ChatGPT/Claude does one job: predict what plausibly comes next, one token at a time. That produces useful writing, critique, and code — but it optimizes for plausible, not true, and sounds equally confident either way.

In real work

Treat it like a brilliant, fast, overconfident intern: great at drafts, options, structure. Never the final authority on facts, sources, or specs.

Why this video: the clearest visual explanation of what's inside an LLM. Watch at least the first 3½ minutes — the "predict, sample, repeat" loop explains every weird AI behaviour you'll ever meet.

2 · Image models don't collage — they synthesize (and that cuts both ways)

Diffusion models turn noise into images, steered by your words. They don't store or paste anyone's image — and they were trained on millions of artists' works, mostly scraped without consent. Both facts are true. The mechanism being non-copying doesn't settle the consent question.

Watch out

The output is statistically average by default. "Make it cyberpunk" returns the mean of every cyberpunk image online. Specific direction is the only escape from average.

Why this video: how image generators went from research toy to artist controversy — the diffusion animation at 5:30 is the part to watch. Made in 2022: the tools are dated, the questions aren't.

3 · The four reliable failure modes

FailureLooks likeYour counter-move
HallucinationInvented facts, fake sources, artists who don't existVerify every name, date, quote, citation
GenericnessCompetent, soulless, like everything elseConstraints, references, your taste
BiasDefault faces, default beauty, default art historyNotice the defaults; direct against them
AmnesiaForgets your brief mid-chat (context window full)Restate constraints; fresh chats

4 · The artist's objections — taken seriously

  • Consent: training data was largely scraped without permission. True. Tools differ (Firefly trains on licensed content) — tool choice is an ethical act.
  • Copyright: purely AI-generated output generally can't be copyrighted — human authorship is the legal test. Your judgment isn't just ethics; it's ownership.
  • Labor: some tasks are getting automated. The defensible ground: taste, judgment, accountability, relationships — this course's syllabus.
  • Slop: generic is now free, so distinctive human work gains value.
  • Disclosure: the EU is making synthetic-media transparency law. Honesty is becoming a hard skill.

5 · The one rule

The course in one line

Use AI for friction. Keep meaning human. Friction = the parts that aren't your art (summaries, options, checklists, file names). Meaning = concept, taste, selection, ethics, final craft, your name on it. Where the line sits is yours to draw — that's today's lab.

04Demo: One Task, Three Ways

I made this, so can you

The instructor runs the same task three ways. Watch what changes — it isn't the model.

Prepared demo artifact

Instructor backup / finished example: comparison-board.html. Use it if the live tool run fails, or to reveal the finished structure after critique.

  1. Weak prompt. "Make a poster concept for an art school degree show." → fast, confident, generic. Could be any college, anywhere.
  2. Creative brief. Same ask + audience, tone, constraints (2-colour riso, typography-led), references, and the line that matters: "Don't pick a winner — that's my job." → five usable directions with risks.
  3. Critique loop. "Direction 3 is weakest. Critique it against my brief, then give two sharper variants." → the model attacks its own genericness, steered by human taste.
What to notice

The human supplied audience, constraints, taste, and the final call. The model supplied speed and volume. That division of labor is the whole course. Adapt it: swap in any project, keep the structure.

05Step-by-Step Workflows

Your first real AI conversation (the non-sucky version)

  1. Give it a role and a job. Not "hi." Who to be, what you're working on.
  2. Paste context. Project, audience, constraints. Nothing private, nothing that isn't yours to share.
  3. Ask for options, not answers. "Five directions with risks" beats "what should I do."
  4. Make it ask first. "Ask clarifying questions before answering" — instantly better output.
  5. React with specifics. "No. 2 is closest but the tone is too corporate" beats "make it better."
  6. Keep your decision. Choose, reject, refine yourself — and note why. That's process documentation.

The 60-second sanity check (before any AI use)

  • Is this task friction or meaning? (Meaning → don't outsource.)
  • Would I be embarrassed if the raw output were shown as mine?
  • Does anything need verification before reuse?
  • Would an honest viewer want to know AI was involved?
  • Whose material am I uploading — and is it mine to upload?

06Prompt Recipes

Copy, adapt, reuse. The copy button grabs the whole recipe.

The practical creative directorI am an NCAD student working in [your track]. I want help with this project: [describe project in 2–3 sentences, with audience and constraints]. Act as a practical creative director, not a replacement artist. Ask clarifying questions first if needed. Help me create options, critique them against my goals, and identify what I should make or revise myself. Don't pick winners — that's my job.
The critique loopHere is my [brief/draft/image]. Critique it against these criteria: [paste 3–5 criteria from your brief]. Be specific and unsentimental. For each weakness: name it, explain why it matters for my audience, and suggest one concrete revision. Do not rewrite the whole thing.
The verification passList every factual claim you just made (names, dates, sources, statistics, artists, tools). For each one, tell me how confident you are and how I could verify it independently. Flag anything you may have invented.
The generic detectorRead this back and tell me: which parts could appear in any art student's work, and which parts could only have come from my specific project? Be brutal about the generic parts.

07Lab: Boundary Map + Manifesto (70 min)

Cold start friendly — this lab needs nothing from before today.

Part A — AI Boundary Map (20 min, paper first)

Three columns; sort your actual creative tasks. Minimum 5 per column. "Ideation" is not a task — "finding type references" is.

Green — AI freelyYellow — AI with careRed — human only
Friction; no authorship at stakeTouches creative territory; needs control + maybe disclosureWhere your voice lives

Part B — Same brief, two tools (30 min, pairs welcome)

  1. Write a 5-line brief for a real project (Creative Director recipe above).
  2. Run it in ChatGPT and Claude — one account each per pair is plenty.
  3. Compare: where do they differ? What did both get generic?
  4. Run the Critique Loop on the better one.
  5. Write down three places the human had to intervene — they feed your manifesto.
Zero-generation track

Do Part B entirely as critique: paste a past project description into both tools and compare their critiques. Same lesson, no generation, equal credit.

Part C — Personal AI Use Manifesto v1 (20 min)

8–12 bullets defining ethical, useful AI use for your practice. Specific enough to act on:

  • ✗ Weak: "I'll use AI responsibly."
  • ✓ Strong: "AI can generate layout options, but the final composition is always drawn by me — and I keep the rejected options as process evidence."
Keep it findable

This is v1. You'll rewrite it in Week 15, and the distance between versions is what the course measures. Photograph it now.

08Integrity Check

The weekly four — same every week
  1. What part of this work requires your taste?
  2. What can AI safely accelerate?
  3. What must be verified before you trust it?
  4. What should be disclosed if this becomes portfolio or client work?

This week's special case: your manifesto itself. If AI helped you draft it — did the bullets stay yours? If you can't tell, that's worth a bullet of its own. Full agreement: Student AI Integrity Pact.
