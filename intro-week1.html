The 10 Principles of Motion Design

Before we open After Effects and start pushing pixels, we need to understand the physics of motion. The software is just a tool; these principles are the rules.

📜 The "Holy Scripture" of Motion

These principles were adapted from classic Disney animation fundamentals (the "12 Principles") to fit modern Motion Design. We use them to make logos, UI, and text feel "alive." If you try to animate without understanding them, your work will feel robotic.

The 10 Core Principles

1. Timing, Spacing & Rhythm

Timing is how long an action takes. Spacing is the distance between frames. Together, they create Rhythm.

Uniform spacing feels mechanical. Varied spacing creates personality and "groove."

2. Eases

The acceleration (Ease Out) and deceleration (Ease In) of movement.

Real objects rarely stop instantly. Easing makes motion feel organic rather than linear.

3. Mass & Weight

Giving objects a sense of physical substance (Inertia).

A bowling ball stops differently than a ping pong ball. You must tell the viewer how "heavy" the object is.

4. Anticipation

A preparatory movement before the main action (e.g., winding up a pitch).

It builds energy and clues the viewer in on what is about to happen.

5. Arcs

Natural movement usually follows a curved path, not a straight line.

Straight lines feel digital and stiff. Arcs make movement feel fluid and high-end.

6. Squash, Stretch & Smears

Deforming an object to show flexibility (Squash/Stretch) or extreme speed (Smears/Blur).

Shows impact or acceleration without needing realistic motion blur.

7. Follow Through

When the main body stops, "loose" parts keep moving. Parts move at different rates (Overlapping).

Prevents everything from stopping abruptly at once, which looks robotic.

8. Exaggeration

Pushing reality further for emphasis or style.

"Real" physics can look boring on a small screen. We exaggerate bounces and speed to make the motion "read" clearly.

9. Secondary Animation

Smaller movements that support the main action (e.g., swaying trees in the background).

Adds texture and detail without distracting from the primary movement.

10. Appeal

The quality of the design itself.

Good motion cannot save a bad design. Composition and typography must be solid before you set a keyframe.

Chapter 1: A Brief History of Motion Graphics

Precursors of Animation

The Early Quest for Motion: Cave paintings in Lascaux (France) and Altamira (Spain) depicted animals with multiple legs to suggest movement. Ancient Egyptians and Greeks implied motion in wall decoration and vessel painting.

Persistence of Vision: The fundamental principle that makes animation possible. The eye retains an image for a fraction of a second after it disappears, blending a rapid succession of stills into a continuous picture.

Optical Devices (17th - 19th Century)

Magic Lantern (1600s)

Projected hand-painted or photographic glass slides using fire/gas light.

Thaumatrope (1820s)

A paper disc on strings. Twirling merges images on front and back. Popularized by Dr. John A. Paris.

Phenakistoscope (1832)

Invented by Joseph Plateau. Two discs on a spindle viewed through a mirror.

Zoetrope (1834)

The "Wheel of Life" by William George Horner. A cylinder with slots -- no mirror required.

Praxinoscope (1877)

Invented by Emile Reynaud. Improved the Zoetrope using mirrors on an inner cylinder.

Demonstration of 19th Century Optical Toys

Early Cinematic Inventions

  • Eadweard Muybridge: Sequential cameras proving horses lift all legs while galloping. Invented the Zoopraxiscope.
  • Lumiere Brothers: Developed the Cinematographe -- first mass-produced camera-printer-projector.
  • Winsor McCay (1914): Created Gertie the Dinosaur -- the first character with an identifiable personality.
  • Earl Hurd (1910): Developed Cel Animation using translucent sheets.
  • Max Fleischer (1917): Patented Rotoscoping. Created Betty Boop and Popeye.
  • Georges Melies: A Trip to the Moon (1902) -- stop-motion for magical transformations.

Winsor McCay's "Gertie the Dinosaur" (1914)

Experimental Animation

Artists rejected classical representation for "Pure Cinema," motivated by art rather than commercial gain.

  • Viking Eggeling & Hans Richter: Abstract symbols and scroll drawings. Symphonie Diagonale (1923).
  • Fernand Leger: Cubist filmmaker. Ballet Mechanique (1923).
  • Oskar Fischinger: "Visual music" pioneer. Composition in Blue. Hired for Disney's Fantasia.
  • Len Lye: "Direct-on-Film" -- scratching and painting on 35mm celluloid. Free Radicals (1958).
  • Norman McLaren: "Poet of Animation" at the National Film Board of Canada. Painted on film.
  • Lotte Reiniger: Silhouette cut-out animation. First full-length animated feature: The Adventures of Prince Achmed (~1926).
  • Jan Svankmajer & The Brothers Quay: Czech surrealism and grimy puppet animation.

Fernand Leger's "Ballet Mechanique" (1923)

Computer Animation Pioneers

  • John Whitney: Built a mechanical analog computer from WWII anti-aircraft sights. Created the title sequence for Vertigo.
  • Ivan Sutherland (1961): Created Sketchpad at MIT -- the first vector drawing program using a light pen.
  • Stan Vanderbeek: Invented the Movie-Drome (360-degree projection). Worked at Bell Labs.
  • Robert Abel: Combined cinematography with CGI for commercials and Disney's Tron.

John Whitney's "Catalogue"

Motion Graphics in Film Titles

  • Saul Bass: Treated titles as a "prologue." Carmen Jones, The Man With the Golden Arm, Anatomy of a Murder, Vertigo.
  • Maurice Binder: The iconic "Gun Barrel" opening for James Bond (Dr. No).
  • Pablo Ferro: Master of the "quick-cut" technique. Dr. Strangelove, A Clockwork Orange.
  • Kyle Cooper: Se7en (1995) -- a landmark in design history. Founded Imaginary Forces.
  • Richard Alan Greenberg: "Flying" titles in Superman (1978), Alien, The Matrix.

Motion Graphics in Television

Harry Marks (ABC/CBS) is considered the father of broadcast motion graphics. He hired Douglas Trumbull (SFX from 2001: A Space Odyssey) to use the Slit Scan camera technique for the ABC Movie of the Week intro. This "moving logo" concept revolutionized TV branding.
